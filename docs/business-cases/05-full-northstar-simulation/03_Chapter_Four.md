# Chapter Four — Mid-Project Recovery

## Purpose

Chapter Four is the Northstar flagship simulation's recovery chapter. It converts the learner's earlier planning and execution choices into visible operational consequences and requires the learner to stabilize the project without abandoning governance, stakeholder trust, or intended outcomes.

The chapter must feel materially different depending on the learner's prior decisions. It is not a generic crisis sequence. It is the point where schedule pressure, cost pressure, quality risk, vendor performance, stakeholder confidence, and regulatory exposure converge.

## Chapter Learning Intent

The learner must demonstrate the ability to:

- diagnose project health using integrated evidence rather than isolated symptoms;
- distinguish risks from active issues;
- apply formal change control under pressure;
- negotiate corrective action with internal teams and external vendors;
- protect compliance and quality when schedule pressure increases;
- recover stakeholder trust through transparent, credible communication;
- update forecasts and commitments using current evidence;
- make trade-offs without hiding consequences;
- preserve decision traceability;
- escalate appropriately without transferring ownership of the problem.

---

## Entry Conditions

Chapter Four begins only after Chapter Three exit conditions are met.

Required inherited state includes:

- the approved or conditionally approved project management baseline;
- the current integrated schedule;
- the current budget and forecast;
- the risk register and issue log;
- the vendor agreement and performance history;
- the quality management approach;
- the stakeholder engagement state;
- all commitments recorded during Chapters One through Three;
- accepted and rejected deliverables;
- unresolved decision consequences;
- current project health metrics.

The engine must not reset trust, risk, cost, schedule, quality, or team-state indicators at chapter entry.

---

## Chapter Opening State

The chapter opens with a project health deterioration signal. The exact severity is determined by prior learner choices.

At least three of the following conditions must be active:

- schedule variance exceeds the accepted tolerance;
- forecast cost increases due to rework, vendor delay, or resource pressure;
- a critical integration dependency is behind plan;
- a regulatory or compliance concern has been raised;
- a major stakeholder no longer trusts the current forecast;
- the team is showing signs of overload or conflict;
- a deliverable has failed quality review;
- the vendor has missed a contractual or operational commitment;
- a late change request threatens the baseline;
- executive leadership is requesting a recovery commitment before evidence is complete.

The learner receives a consolidated project health alert and is informed that a steering committee review has been accelerated.

---

## Canonical Narrative Spine

Chapter Four follows this required narrative sequence.

### 1. Project Health Alert

The learner receives a consolidated warning showing that the project is no longer operating within one or more agreed tolerances.

The alert must include evidence from multiple domains:

- schedule;
- cost;
- scope;
- quality;
- risk;
- resources;
- stakeholder confidence;
- vendor performance.

The learner must not be allowed to resolve the alert by acknowledging it only. A diagnosis activity is required.

### 2. Recovery Diagnosis

The learner reviews current project information and identifies:

- root causes;
- active issues;
- secondary effects;
- invalid assumptions;
- decisions requiring escalation;
- recovery options;
- information gaps.

The simulation should reward evidence-based diagnosis and penalize solutions that treat only visible symptoms.

### 3. Vendor Performance Escalation

The external vendor reports an inability to meet a key integration or delivery commitment under the current assumptions.

Depending on prior state, the vendor may:

- accept responsibility;
- dispute the baseline;
- blame delayed client inputs;
- request additional funding;
- propose a reduced scope;
- offer a phased recovery plan;
- escalate commercially.

The learner must decide how to respond while preserving leverage, working relationships, and contractual discipline.

### 4. Compliance and Quality Concern

A compliance, privacy, safety, or quality concern is raised regarding the proposed recovery path.

The concern must make it impossible to recover the project solely through schedule compression.

The learner must choose whether to:

- pause affected work;
- authorize targeted remediation;
- seek specialist review;
- revise acceptance criteria;
- reject an unsafe shortcut;
- escalate a residual risk for explicit acceptance.

### 5. Formal Change Request

A senior stakeholder introduces a late request that appears strategically valuable but materially affects scope, time, cost, risk, or quality.

The request must be processed through integrated change control.

The learner must:

- clarify the requested outcome;
- assess impact;
- identify options;
- document assumptions;
- recommend approve, reject, defer, or modify;
- identify required governance authority;
- update affected baselines only after authorization.

### 6. Team Conflict and Capacity Pressure

Internal delivery teams disagree about the recovery approach.

At minimum, the conflict involves competing priorities such as:

- delivery speed versus quality;
- business urgency versus technical feasibility;
- compliance assurance versus operational convenience;
- resource sustainability versus executive urgency;
- local optimization versus integrated project outcomes.

The learner must select an appropriate conflict response and produce a clear path forward.

### 7. Recovery Plan Development

The learner creates or approves a recovery plan containing:

- root-cause summary;
- current project health statement;
- corrective actions;
- preventive actions;
- owners;
- due dates;
- dependencies;
- cost and schedule impact;
- risk implications;
- quality and compliance protections;
- communication commitments;
- escalation triggers;
- revised forecast.

The recovery plan must not automatically imply a revised baseline. Baseline changes require appropriate approval.

### 8. Steering Committee Recovery Review

The learner presents the recovery recommendation to the steering committee.

The committee challenges:

- forecast credibility;
- funding implications;
- accountability;
- business-value protection;
- change-control discipline;
- regulatory exposure;
- vendor commitments;
- stakeholder communication.

The learner must make a recommendation and answer objections using evidence.

### 9. Recovery Authorization

The steering committee produces one of the following states:

- recovery plan approved;
- recovery plan approved with conditions;
- revised plan required;
- partial recovery approved;
- scope reduction authorized;
- additional funding authorized;
- escalation to sponsor or governance authority required.

The authorization outcome becomes a binding state transition.

### 10. Stabilization Confirmation

The chapter closes after the learner demonstrates that the project has moved from uncontrolled deterioration to a governed recovery state.

The project does not need to be fully healthy. It must be demonstrably controlled, transparent, and executable.

## Required Meetings

### Recovery Triage Meeting

Purpose:

- establish a shared understanding of current project health;
- separate facts from assumptions;
- assign immediate containment actions;
- determine what must be escalated.

Required participants:

- learner project manager;
- delivery lead;
- technical lead;
- vendor lead;
- quality or compliance representative;
- business owner or product representative.

Required outputs:

- confirmed problem statement;
- immediate containment actions;
- named owners;
- information gaps;
- escalation list.

### Vendor Recovery Meeting

Purpose:

- confront missed commitments;
- validate accountability;
- negotiate a feasible recovery path;
- preserve contractual and relationship options.

Required outputs:

- vendor corrective-action proposal;
- revised commitments;
- commercial implications;
- unresolved disputes;
- escalation trigger.

### Change Control Review

Purpose:

- evaluate the late change request;
- compare options;
- identify integrated impacts;
- determine disposition authority.

Required outputs:

- impact assessment;
- recommendation;
- approval route;
- decision record.

### Steering Committee Recovery Review

Purpose:

- authorize or reject the recovery direction;
- confirm accountability and governance;
- align leadership expectations;
- protect project value.

Required outputs:

- formal recovery decision;
- conditions of approval;
- authorized tolerances;
- communication direction;
- next review date.

## Required Inbox Events

The inbox sequence must include:

1. project health escalation notice;
2. vendor delay or cost-impact notification;
3. compliance or quality concern;
4. late change request from a senior stakeholder;
5. team capacity or conflict escalation;
6. steering committee request for recovery recommendation;
7. recovery authorization or conditional approval notice.

Informational messages must be distinguishable from decision-bearing messages.

Only messages with an explicit action contract may create pending decisions.

- distinguish risks from issues;
- prioritize containment actions;

## Canonical Decision Classes

### Decision 1 — Immediate Containment

The learner chooses how to prevent further deterioration while diagnosis continues.

Possible approaches include:

- targeted work pause;
- continued work with additional controls;
- temporary resource reallocation;
- immediate executive escalation;
- no containment.

Strong decisions are proportionate, evidence-based, time-bounded, and explicit about ownership.

### Decision 2 — Vendor Response

The learner chooses how to respond to vendor underperformance.

Possible approaches include:

- accept the vendor recovery proposal;
- negotiate revised commitments;
- issue a formal corrective-action requirement;
- invoke contractual remedies;
- replace part of the vendor scope;
- escalate commercially;
- absorb the impact internally.

The engine must evaluate both delivery effectiveness and relationship consequences.

### Decision 3 — Compliance or Quality Protection

The learner determines whether and how to protect compliance or quality during recovery.

Strong decisions reject undocumented shortcuts and make residual risk visible to the correct authority.

### Decision 4 — Change Request Disposition

The learner recommends:

- approve;
- approve with modification;
- defer;
- reject;
- split into a later release;
- request additional analysis.

The decision is invalid if baselines are changed before authorization.

### Decision 5 — Team Conflict Response

The learner selects a conflict-management approach appropriate to urgency, authority, and relationship needs.

The engine must distinguish productive collaboration from avoidance disguised as consensus.

### Decision 6 — Recovery Strategy

The learner recommends a recovery strategy such as:

- protect scope and extend schedule;
- protect date and reduce scope;
- increase investment;
- phase delivery;
- recover through sequencing changes;
- terminate or replace a failing component;
- combine multiple controlled actions.

The selected strategy must expose trade-offs.

### Decision 7 — Steering Committee Commitment

The learner decides what commitment to make to leadership.

Strong performance avoids unsupported guarantees and communicates confidence ranges, assumptions, and triggers.

## Delayed Consequences from Earlier Chapters

Chapter Four must activate previously seeded consequences.

Examples include:

- an undocumented assumption becoming a schedule issue;
- weak stakeholder engagement causing resistance during recovery;
- an aggressive estimate reducing forecast credibility;
- incomplete acceptance criteria creating rework;
- an informal vendor concession weakening contractual leverage;
- failure to record a commitment creating conflict;
- premature escalation reducing team trust;
- weak risk ownership causing delayed response;
- strong planning discipline reducing recovery effort;
- transparent communication increasing executive support.

The engine must link each delayed consequence to its originating decision or state transition.

## Stakeholder Behavior

### Executive Sponsor

The sponsor seeks confidence, accountability, and protection of strategic value.

Behavior changes:

- becomes more directive when earlier forecasts were unreliable;
- provides political support when the learner has been transparent;
- pressures for certainty when executive confidence is low;
- accepts phased recovery when trade-offs are credible.

### Business Owner

The business owner protects operational outcomes and may resist scope reduction.

Behavior changes:

- collaborates when impacts are explained early;
- escalates when requested outcomes are reframed without consent;
- supports deferral when value is preserved through sequencing.

### Technical Lead

The technical lead emphasizes feasibility, integration integrity, and sustainable recovery.

Behavior changes:

- becomes defensive if prior warnings were ignored;
- supports the learner when technical evidence is represented accurately;
- resists schedule commitments that rely on hidden technical debt.

### Clinical or Operational Leader

This stakeholder protects operational readiness, safety, and adoption.

Behavior changes:

- rejects recovery paths that reduce readiness or safety controls;
- supports phased deployment when operational risk is acknowledged;
- loses trust when disruption is minimized or concealed.

### Compliance or Quality Lead

This stakeholder requires evidence, traceability, and explicit risk ownership.

Behavior changes:

- escalates when shortcuts are proposed;
- collaborates when brought into analysis early;
- supports controlled exceptions only with proper authority and documentation.

### Vendor Lead

The vendor lead balances delivery recovery, commercial exposure, and reputation.

Behavior changes:

- becomes cooperative when accountability is shared fairly;
- becomes adversarial when blamed without evidence;
- offers stronger commitments when acceptance criteria and dependencies are clear;
- escalates commercially when informal scope growth is treated as included work.

### Finance Representative

The finance representative requires forecast credibility and visibility into cost exposure.

Behavior changes:

- challenges unquantified recovery plans;
- supports additional funding when benefits and controls are clear;
- resists contingency use without traceability.

### Team Members

The team responds to workload, decision clarity, and perceived fairness.

Behavior changes:

- engagement improves when priorities are reduced and ownership is clear;
- burnout risk increases when recovery depends on sustained overtime;
- conflict increases when leadership gives contradictory direction.

## Project Health Effects

Chapter Four decisions may affect:

- schedule confidence;
- cost forecast;
- scope stability;
- quality confidence;
- risk exposure;
- compliance confidence;
- vendor confidence;
- team sustainability;
- stakeholder trust;
- executive confidence;
- business-value confidence;
- governance integrity.

No single decision should produce only positive effects. Recovery choices must contain realistic trade-offs.

## Experience-Level Tailoring

### Explorer

Explorer mode provides:

- guided diagnosis prompts;
- plain-language definitions of risk, issue, corrective action, and change control;
- visible impact categories;
- limited recovery options;
- warnings before unauthorized baseline changes;
- structured steering committee preparation.

Explorer mode must not select the answer for the learner.

### Practitioner

Practitioner mode provides:

- incomplete but sufficient evidence;
- moderate ambiguity;
- multiple viable recovery options;
- stakeholder disagreement;
- consequences that require integrated analysis;
- limited coaching before decisions.

### Leader

Leader mode provides:

- conflicting executive priorities;
- incomplete or politically framed information;
- contractual ambiguity;
- pressure for unsupported certainty;
- competing stakeholder coalitions;
- second-order effects;
- accountability for strategic recommendation and governance quality.

Leader mode may include no fully optimal answer. Performance is evaluated on reasoning quality, transparency, and controlled execution.

## AI Mentor Role

The AI mentor may:

- ask the learner to separate symptoms from causes;
- challenge unsupported assumptions;
- point out missing impact categories;
- ask who owns a residual risk;
- remind the learner that approval authority matters;
- prompt reflection on stakeholder trust;
- compare stated commitments with recorded evidence.

The AI mentor must not:

- reveal the scoring model;
- identify the highest-scoring option before submission;
- invent project facts;
- override governance authority;
- erase consequences from earlier choices.

## Failure Patterns

The following patterns must produce meaningful negative consequences:

- promising recovery before diagnosis;
- hiding schedule or cost deterioration;
- treating active issues as future risks;
- bypassing change control;
- compressing schedule without quality analysis;
- shifting all responsibility to the vendor;
- accepting a vendor plan without measurable commitments;
- approving a change without impact analysis;
- using sustained overtime as the primary recovery method;
- presenting a single forecast as certain despite major assumptions;
- changing baselines before authorization;
- escalating every conflict rather than managing it;
- minimizing compliance concerns;
- failing to update decision and governance records.

---

## Strong Performance Patterns

Strong learner performance includes:

- diagnosing before committing;
- making immediate containment actions explicit;
- distinguishing root cause from symptom;
- quantifying impacts and uncertainty;
- preserving compliance and quality controls;
- negotiating accountable vendor commitments;
- presenting alternatives with visible trade-offs;
- maintaining transparent stakeholder communication;
- using the correct approval authority;
- updating baselines only after approval;
- protecting the team from unsustainable recovery expectations;
- documenting commitments and escalation triggers;
- producing a credible, executable recovery plan.

## Chapter Exit Conditions

Chapter Four is complete only when all required conditions are met.

Required exit conditions:

1. the primary project-health deterioration has been diagnosed;
2. immediate containment actions are recorded;
3. active issues and recovery risks are updated;
4. the vendor response has been decided and documented;
5. the compliance or quality concern has a governed disposition;
6. the late change request has been processed through change control;
7. the team conflict has been addressed;
8. a recovery plan has been created;
9. the steering committee has issued a formal decision;
10. the project forecast has been updated;
11. all approved changes are reflected in authoritative state;
12. stakeholder communication commitments are recorded;
13. the project has returned to a controlled recovery state.

The chapter must remain incomplete when a required decision exists only in local UI state or when authorization has not been persisted.

---

## Chapter Exit States

Possible canonical exit states include:

### Controlled Recovery

The project remains under pressure but has an approved and credible path forward.

### Conditional Recovery

The project may proceed only after specific conditions are satisfied.

### Phased Recovery

The project continues through reduced, staged, or sequenced delivery.

### Scope-Reduced Recovery

Leadership protects the target date or critical outcome by reducing authorized scope.

### Investment-Supported Recovery

Additional funding or resources are authorized with explicit controls.

### Escalated Recovery

The project cannot be stabilized within the learner's authority and requires higher governance action.

No exit state may erase the underlying consequences that remain relevant to Chapters Five and Six.

## Transition to Chapter Five

Chapter Five begins with the project operating under the recovery decision established here.

The next chapter must inherit:

- the approved recovery strategy;
- revised or unchanged baselines;
- current cost and schedule forecast;
- vendor commitments;
- unresolved residual risks;
- current stakeholder trust;
- team capacity state;
- compliance and quality conditions;
- approved scope changes;
- steering committee conditions;
- all recovery communication commitments.

Chapter Five should test whether the recovery plan can survive renewed delivery pressure, readiness demands, acceptance decisions, and transition planning.
