## 1. Purpose

Chapter Two converts the authorized Northstar Connected Care initiative into an integrated, reviewable, and conditionally executable delivery plan.

The chapter must force the learner to move beyond high-level intent and establish the governance, scope, sequencing, stakeholder alignment, risk posture, quality approach, procurement strategy, and communications discipline required for responsible execution.

This chapter is not a checklist exercise. The learner must reconcile competing expectations, incomplete evidence, healthcare constraints, vendor dependencies, and executive pressure while producing a credible planning baseline.

---

## 2. Narrative Position

### Entry condition

Chapter Two begins after Chapter One has established:

- strategic authorization;
- initial project charter;
- preliminary stakeholder identification;
- delivery approach selection;
- initial assumptions, constraints, and risks;
- learner authority to proceed into planning.

### Exit condition

Chapter Two ends when the learner presents an integrated planning baseline for approval.

The baseline may be:

- approved;
- approved with conditions;
- deferred pending corrective work;
- rejected because material planning gaps remain.

Any residual risks, stakeholder objections, assumptions, or unresolved dependencies must carry into Chapter Three.

---

## 3. Chapter Objectives

The learner must:

1. translate the business case into an integrated delivery plan;
2. define and control project scope;
3. establish governance and decision rights;
4. build a realistic schedule and milestone structure;
5. define risk and issue management expectations;
6. plan quality, testing, and acceptance;
7. establish stakeholder and communications approaches;
8. develop procurement and vendor onboarding plans;
9. define resource and operating assumptions;
10. prepare the planning baseline for formal approval.

---

## 4. Learning Outcomes

By the end of Chapter Two, the learner should demonstrate the ability to:

- tailor planning depth to project risk and context;
- integrate scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, procurement, and stakeholder planning;
- distinguish assumptions from commitments;
- identify dependencies and ownership gaps;
- communicate uncertainty without creating false confidence;
- build governance that enables timely decisions;
- plan for healthcare safety, privacy, operational continuity, and responsible technology use;
- present a defensible baseline rather than an artificially perfect plan.

---

## 5. Workplace State

The workplace is collaborative but increasingly political and demanding.

Stakeholders are willing to participate, but they seek influence over scope, schedule, governance, and success measures.

The learner experiences:

- conflicting priorities;
- pressure for early delivery dates;
- incomplete requirements;
- vendor assumptions presented as facts;
- operational constraints that were understated during initiation;
- concerns about privacy, clinical safety, adoption, and support readiness;
- differing views of what “success” means.

The learner’s credibility begins to depend on whether planning decisions are transparent, evidence-based, and integrated.

---

## 6. Chapter Structure

Chapter Two is organized into five narrative movements.

### Movement 1 — Planning kickoff

The learner convenes the planning effort, confirms planning responsibilities, and establishes working norms.

Key tensions:

- who owns which planning decisions;
- whether planning is treated as a shared effort or delegated to the project manager alone;
- whether executive expectations are realistic;
- whether unresolved Chapter One assumptions are explicitly surfaced.

### Movement 2 — Scope and requirements alignment

The learner must reconcile executive ambition, clinical needs, technical feasibility, privacy requirements, and operational constraints.

Key tensions:

- minimum viable scope versus strategic completeness;
- patient and clinician needs versus schedule pressure;
- business value versus technology complexity;
- whether ambiguous requirements are baselined prematurely.

### Movement 3 — Integrated planning

The learner develops the schedule, risk approach, quality strategy, communications plan, procurement plan, and governance model as one connected system.

Key tensions:

- dependencies between internal teams and vendors;
- resource availability;
- confidence levels in estimates;
- the difference between target dates and committed dates;
- whether quality and readiness work is built into the plan or deferred.

### Movement 4 — Plan challenge and revision

Stakeholders challenge the integrated plan.

The learner must respond to objections, identify real gaps, reject unsupported demands, and revise the baseline where evidence supports change.

### Movement 5 — Planning approval

The learner presents the final integrated baseline to the steering group.

Approval depends on the quality of the plan, treatment of residual risk, stakeholder alignment, and the learner’s ability to communicate trade-offs.

## 7. Canonical Events

Chapter Two must contain the following major events.

### Event 2.1 — Planning kickoff workshop

**Purpose:** Establish the planning process, workstreams, responsibilities, and decision cadence.

**Required outputs:**

- planning responsibility matrix;
- working assumptions log;
- initial planning calendar;
- decision and escalation path.

### Event 2.2 — Scope and requirements workshop

**Purpose:** Align strategic objectives with clinical, operational, technical, privacy, and adoption requirements.

**Required outputs:**

- scope statement draft;
- requirements categories;
- exclusions and deferred scope;
- unresolved requirement conflicts.

### Event 2.3 — Governance design session

**Purpose:** Confirm decision rights, approval thresholds, escalation paths, and reporting expectations.

**Required outputs:**

- governance model;
- decision authority matrix;
- steering committee cadence;
- change-control approach.

### Event 2.4 — Risk and dependency workshop

**Purpose:** Identify major uncertainty, external dependencies, regulatory concerns, vendor assumptions, and operational constraints.

**Required outputs:**

- expanded risk register;
- dependency map;
- risk owners;
- escalation triggers.

### Event 2.5 — Schedule and resource planning session

**Purpose:** Develop milestone sequencing, resource assumptions, vendor dependencies, and realistic timing.

**Required outputs:**

- milestone schedule;
- critical dependencies;
- resource constraints;
- confidence assumptions.

### Event 2.6 — Quality and acceptance planning

**Purpose:** Define how solution quality, clinical safety, privacy, testing, operational readiness, and acceptance will be demonstrated.

**Required outputs:**

- quality strategy;
- acceptance criteria framework;
- test and readiness approach;
- responsible technology checkpoints.

### Event 2.7 — Procurement and vendor planning review

**Purpose:** Confirm vendor scope, responsibilities, onboarding needs, commercial dependencies, and performance expectations.

**Required outputs:**

- procurement plan;
- vendor responsibility matrix;
- contract assumptions;
- performance and escalation expectations.

### Event 2.8 — Integrated plan challenge

**Purpose:** Test the credibility of the planning baseline before formal approval.

**Required outputs:**

- challenged assumptions;
- required revisions;
- accepted residual risks;
- unresolved objections.

### Event 2.9 — Planning baseline approval

**Purpose:** Secure formal authorization to begin execution.

**Possible outcomes:**

- approved;
- approved with conditions;
- deferred;
- rejected.

## 13. Planning Tensions

The chapter must include credible tension around:

- target date versus credible schedule;
- broad scope versus minimum viable scope;
- vendor optimism versus internal readiness;
- speed versus clinical and operational safety;
- executive simplicity versus delivery complexity;
- fixed commitments versus evolving requirements;
- cost pressure versus contingency;
- centralized control versus empowered workstreams;
- planning completeness versus adaptive execution.

The learner should not be rewarded merely for choosing the most conservative option. Strong performance requires proportionate planning, explicit assumptions, clear governance, and credible trade-off communication.

## 14. Narrative Decision Points

Phase 1 defines the narrative conditions for the following decision classes. Exact mechanics belong to BC-006 Phase 2.

### Decision 2.A — Scope baseline strategy

The learner must decide how to structure the initial scope baseline under incomplete requirements.

### Decision 2.B — Governance and escalation model

The learner must define who can decide, approve, escalate, and change major project commitments.

### Decision 2.C — Launch target communication

The learner must respond to pressure for a fixed launch date before all estimates and dependencies are mature.

### Decision 2.D — Quality and acceptance threshold

The learner must determine how quality, safety, privacy, and readiness evidence will support acceptance.

### Decision 2.E — Vendor responsibility boundary

The learner must resolve disputed assumptions about vendor and internal responsibilities.

### Decision 2.F — Planning baseline approval recommendation

The learner must recommend approval, conditional approval, deferral, or rejection based on the integrated evidence.

## 15. Immediate and Delayed Consequences

Chapter Two decisions should influence:

- schedule confidence;
- scope clarity;
- stakeholder trust;
- vendor confidence;
- governance confidence;
- risk exposure;
- quality confidence;
- operational readiness;
- budget confidence;
- execution friction in Chapter Three;
- severity of disruption in Chapter Four;
- readiness pressure in Chapter Five;
- closure credibility in Chapter Six.

Examples of delayed consequences include:

- poorly defined responsibility boundaries causing Chapter Three coordination failures;
- optimistic schedule commitments creating Chapter Four executive escalation;
- weak acceptance criteria increasing Chapter Five defect and readiness disputes;
- ignored adoption concerns reducing training effectiveness and stakeholder support;
- incomplete privacy planning creating later compliance pressure;
- strong governance reducing recovery time when risks materialize.

## 16. Experience-Level Tailoring

### Explorer

- explicit planning guidance;
- visible evidence prompts;
- clear distinction between assumptions, risks, and commitments;
- stronger coaching before approval decisions;
- simplified stakeholder ambiguity.

### Practitioner

- moderate guidance;
- competing evidence;
- realistic trade-offs;
- limited consequence previews;
- balanced stakeholder disagreement.

### Leader

- reduced scaffolding;
- incomplete and conflicting evidence;
- strong executive pressure;
- complex responsibility gaps;
- higher consequence intensity;
- less obvious distinction between defensible and weak options.

The core story and canonical events remain the same across all levels.

The chapter is complete only when:

- required planning activities are completed;
- required decisions are resolved;

## 19. Chapter Exit States

### Strong exit

- integrated plan is credible;
- scope and governance are clear;
- major risks have owners;
- vendor responsibilities are explicit;
- quality and readiness are planned early;
- stakeholders understand trade-offs;
- approval is granted with manageable residual risk.

### Conditional exit

- plan is approved with explicit corrective actions;
- some assumptions remain unresolved;
- one or more stakeholders remain concerned;
- schedule or cost confidence is moderate;
- Chapter Three begins with visible conditions.

### Weak exit

- planning baseline contains material gaps;
- commitments exceed available evidence;
- responsibilities remain ambiguous;
- key quality, privacy, adoption, or operational work is deferred;
- approval is deferred or politically forced despite weak confidence.

A weak exit must increase execution friction rather than block the simulation entirely unless the narrative explicitly requires corrective work before progression.

---

## 20. Transition to Chapter Three

Chapter Three begins with team mobilization and execution against the approved or conditionally approved baseline.

The transition must carry forward:

- approved scope and exclusions;
- schedule commitments;
- unresolved assumptions;
- active risks and dependencies;
- governance rules;
- vendor responsibilities;
- stakeholder trust states;
- quality and acceptance expectations;
- corrective actions attached to planning approval;
- any politically imposed commitments that lack sufficient evidence.

The first execution events should expose the quality of the Chapter Two planning work.
