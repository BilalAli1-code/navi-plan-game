# BC-006 Phase 1 — Chapter Three: Early Execution

**Status:** Draft for implementation  
**Parent issue:** BC-006 — Complete Northstar Flagship Simulation (#81)  
**Scope:** Canonical narrative specification for Chapter Three of the Northstar Connected Care simulation.

---

## 1. Purpose

Chapter Three begins active delivery and tests whether the Chapter Two planning baseline is executable under real workplace pressure.

The learner must mobilize teams, onboard vendors, coordinate first deliverables, respond to early quality and communication problems, and preserve stakeholder confidence while maintaining disciplined governance.

This chapter must make earlier planning choices visible through execution outcomes. Strong planning should reduce friction without eliminating complexity. Weak planning should produce realistic but recoverable consequences.

---

## 2. Chapter Identity

**Chapter title:** Early Execution  
**Narrative period:** Project Months 4–5  
**Learner sessions:** Sessions 4 and 5  
**Primary lifecycle emphasis:** Delivery mobilization, team leadership, quality management, communications, vendor management, stakeholder engagement, monitoring, and corrective action.

**Chapter-ending milestone:** First major deliverable accepted or conditionally accepted, with project health clearly shaped by the learner’s planning and execution choices.

---

## 3. Starting Conditions

Chapter Three starts after the integrated planning baseline is approved.

The learner inherits:

- an approved scope baseline;
- an approved schedule and milestone plan;
- a documented governance model;
- a stakeholder engagement strategy;
- an initial risk register;
- a procurement and vendor plan;
- a quality management approach;
- a communications plan;
- explicit planning conditions and residual risks;
- stakeholder trust levels established in Chapters One and Two.

The simulation must preserve all unresolved conditions from earlier chapters.

---

## 4. Chapter Objectives

The learner must:

1. Mobilize internal and external delivery teams.
2. Establish a functioning execution cadence.
3. Confirm role clarity and decision authority.
4. Activate vendor and procurement commitments.
5. Protect the approved baseline while responding to emerging information.
6. Manage early quality concerns before they become systemic.
7. Resolve communication and coordination breakdowns.
8. Maintain credible stakeholder communication.
9. Apply corrective action without bypassing governance.
10. Deliver the first major project output for acceptance.

---

## 5. Learning Outcomes

By the end of the chapter, the learner should be able to:

- translate an approved plan into coordinated execution;
- identify when an execution problem is caused by planning, behavior, governance, or external constraints;
- distinguish risks, issues, defects, assumptions, and changes;
- lead cross-functional collaboration without over-centralizing decisions;
- manage vendor performance against agreed commitments;
- use quality criteria and evidence to guide acceptance;
- communicate project health honestly under pressure;
- select proportionate corrective action;
- preserve psychological safety while maintaining accountability;
- recognize how early decisions generate delayed consequences.

---

## 6. Narrative Summary

Execution begins with visible optimism. Teams are eager to start, executives expect momentum, and the vendor presents an aggressive mobilization plan.

Within the first several weeks, hidden dependencies and coordination gaps emerge. Clinical representatives struggle to attend design sessions. The integration team discovers that a key interface assumption is incomplete. The vendor interprets one requirement differently from the internal team. A first deliverable is produced quickly but does not fully satisfy agreed quality criteria.

The learner must determine whether to accept the output, request remediation, escalate, or adjust the delivery approach. The chapter culminates in a formal first-deliverable review where the learner’s earlier planning decisions and current leadership behavior directly affect the outcome.

---

## 7. Workplace State

The Chapter Three workplace should feel:

- active and fast-moving;
- optimistic but increasingly fragmented;
- dependent on cross-functional cooperation;
- sensitive to communication delays;
- exposed to vendor and technical interpretation risk;
- influenced by learner credibility;
- pressured by executive expectations for visible progress.

The learner should experience a meaningful increase in workload and information volume.

---

## 8. Primary Narrative Threads

### 8.1 Team Mobilization

The learner must establish practical working norms across clinical, technical, operational, vendor, and governance groups.

Key tensions:

- unclear ownership of cross-functional tasks;
- uneven participation;
- conflicting departmental priorities;
- ambiguity between project and operational responsibilities;
- pressure to move before all dependencies are resolved.

### 8.2 Vendor Onboarding

The vendor begins delivery under the approved contract and onboarding plan.

Key tensions:

- vendor interpretation of requirements;
- resource availability;
- reporting quality;
- response times;
- responsibility boundaries;
- commercial pressure to demonstrate progress.

### 8.3 First Deliverables

The project produces initial design, configuration, integration, or workflow outputs.

The learner must evaluate whether outputs satisfy:

- scope expectations;
- quality criteria;
- clinical requirements;
- privacy and security requirements;
- architectural standards;
- acceptance evidence.

### 8.4 Communication Breakdown

A critical dependency is not communicated consistently across teams.

The learner must identify:

- who knew what;
- when information became available;
- whether the communications plan was followed;
- whether escalation thresholds were clear;
- what corrective action is proportionate.

### 8.5 Early Quality Concern

A deliverable appears complete but contains material gaps.

The learner must resist superficial completion and use evidence to decide whether the deliverable is acceptable, conditionally acceptable, or rejected.

### 8.6 Stakeholder Confidence

Stakeholders begin judging whether the learner can lead under pressure.

Credibility is affected by:

- clarity of communication;
- willingness to surface bad news;
- follow-through on commitments;
- fairness in accountability;
- consistency with governance;
- responsiveness to concerns.

## 9. Canonical Event Sequence

### Event 3.1 — Execution Kickoff

**Purpose:** Move the project from planning into coordinated delivery.

**Participants:** Learner, sponsor, product owner, clinical lead, technical lead, operations lead, PMO representative, vendor delivery lead.

**Outputs:**

- confirmed execution cadence;
- confirmed workstream ownership;
- confirmed escalation routes;
- immediate mobilization actions;
- open dependency list.

### Event 3.2 — Vendor Mobilization Concern

The vendor reports a staffing substitution and proposes a revised onboarding sequence.

The learner must assess whether the change:

- remains contractually acceptable;
- affects schedule confidence;
- affects required capabilities;
- requires formal approval;
- should trigger an escalation.

### Event 3.3 — Clinical Availability Conflict

Clinical stakeholders cannot consistently attend design workshops because of operational demands.

The learner must balance:

- project schedule;
- patient-care responsibilities;
- decision quality;
- stakeholder fatigue;
- alternative engagement methods.

### Event 3.4 — Integration Assumption Failure

The technical team discovers that a critical interface assumption is incomplete or incorrect.

This must be treated as a combined technical, schedule, risk, and stakeholder issue rather than a purely technical problem.

### Event 3.5 — Communication Breakdown Review

A dependency was not shared with all affected teams, creating rework.

The learner must facilitate a review that focuses on system improvement and accountability rather than blame.

### Event 3.6 — First Quality Review

The first major deliverable is evaluated against the approved quality plan and acceptance criteria.

Possible findings:

- fully compliant;
- minor correctable gaps;
- material quality concerns;
- incomplete evidence;
- conflicting stakeholder interpretations.

### Event 3.7 — Executive Progress Pressure

The sponsor or executive steering group requests a positive progress message despite unresolved quality or schedule concerns.

The learner must communicate accurately while maintaining confidence and forward momentum.

### Event 3.8 — First Major Deliverable Decision

The learner recommends one of the following:

- accept;
- conditionally accept with documented remediation;
- reject and remediate;
- escalate for executive decision where authority is insufficient.

The outcome determines the Chapter Three exit state.

## 10. Required Meetings

### 10.1 Execution Kickoff Meeting

**Purpose:** Confirm mobilization, ownership, cadence, dependencies, and escalation routes.

**Required preparation:**

- approved integrated plan;
- responsibility assignment information;
- milestone schedule;
- communications plan;
- risk register;
- vendor onboarding plan.

**Required outcomes:**

- delivery workstreams activated;
- meeting cadence confirmed;
- first dependency review scheduled;
- unresolved ownership gaps assigned.

### 10.2 Vendor Mobilization Review

**Purpose:** Evaluate staffing, onboarding, reporting, and contractual alignment.

**Required outcomes:**

- accepted or revised mobilization plan;
- documented responsibility boundaries;
- confirmed vendor reporting cadence;
- escalation or remediation actions where required.

### 10.3 Cross-Functional Dependency Review

**Purpose:** Surface hidden dependencies and confirm ownership.

**Required outcomes:**

- updated dependency log;
- updated schedule assumptions;
- newly identified risks and issues;
- explicit owners and due dates.

### 10.4 Quality Review Meeting

**Purpose:** Evaluate the first major deliverable against agreed criteria.

**Required outcomes:**

- documented quality findings;
- acceptance recommendation;
- remediation actions;
- evidence gaps;
- responsible owners.

### 10.5 Executive Progress Review

**Purpose:** Present an accurate view of delivery progress, risks, quality, and confidence.

**Required outcomes:**

- accepted status narrative;
- documented executive direction;
- escalation decisions;
- confirmed next milestone expectations.

## 11. Inbox and Communication Flow

Chapter Three communications must include a mix of informational, actionable, and decision-triggering items.

### Informational items

- vendor onboarding confirmation;
- project team introductions;
- updated operating schedules;
- routine PMO reporting reminders;
- workstream status summaries.

### Actionable items

- request to confirm workshop attendance;
- request to resolve an ownership gap;
- request to update the risk register;
- request to review vendor staffing changes;
- request to prepare quality evidence.

### Decision-triggering items

- vendor substitution approval request;
- clinical participation escalation;
- scope interpretation conflict;
- first deliverable acceptance recommendation;
- executive request to compress remediation time.

Informational messages must never be counted as decisions.

## 13. Required Activities

### Activity 3.1 — Mobilize Delivery Teams

**Prerequisites:** Approved planning baseline.  
**Completion rule:** Workstream ownership, cadence, and immediate actions confirmed.  
**Learning outcome:** Translate plans into coordinated execution.

### Activity 3.2 — Review Vendor Mobilization

**Prerequisites:** Vendor onboarding information received.  
**Completion rule:** Staffing, responsibilities, reporting, and escalation routes reviewed.  
**Learning outcome:** Apply procurement governance during execution.

### Activity 3.3 — Conduct Dependency Review

**Prerequisites:** Initial workstream activity underway.  
**Completion rule:** Critical dependencies documented with owners and timing.  
**Learning outcome:** Use systems thinking to identify cross-functional exposure.

### Activity 3.4 — Respond to Communication Breakdown

**Prerequisites:** Rework or missed dependency discovered.  
**Completion rule:** Root cause, corrective action, and communication improvements documented.  
**Learning outcome:** Improve team systems without defaulting to blame.

### Activity 3.5 — Evaluate First Deliverable

**Prerequisites:** Deliverable and quality evidence available.  
**Completion rule:** Acceptance recommendation supported by evidence.  
**Learning outcome:** Apply quality criteria and professional judgment.

### Activity 3.6 — Provide Executive Status Update

**Prerequisites:** Current project health reviewed.  
**Completion rule:** Accurate, concise, decision-useful communication delivered.  
**Learning outcome:** Maintain confidence without hiding uncertainty.

### Activity 3.7 — Complete Chapter Reflection

**Prerequisites:** First deliverable decision completed.  
**Completion rule:** Learner identifies one planning assumption, one leadership behavior, and one governance mechanism that affected execution.  
**Learning outcome:** Connect action, consequence, and continuous improvement.

## 14. Decision Classes

Phase 1 defines narrative decision classes. Exact mechanics belong to later BC-006 phases.

### 14.1 Vendor Staffing Decision

The learner decides whether to:

- accept the substitution;
- accept with conditions;
- reject and enforce the original commitment;
- escalate for commercial review.

### 14.2 Clinical Participation Decision

The learner decides how to maintain clinical input while respecting operational constraints.

### 14.3 Integration Response Decision

The learner selects a response to the failed interface assumption.

### 14.4 Communication Breakdown Response

The learner determines corrective action, accountability, and process improvement.

### 14.5 Deliverable Acceptance Decision

The learner decides whether evidence supports acceptance, conditional acceptance, rejection, or escalation.

### 14.6 Executive Communication Decision

The learner determines how to communicate progress when leaders want certainty that the evidence does not support.

## 15. Delayed Consequence Seeds

Chapter Three must establish consequences that may become visible in Chapters Four and Five.

Examples:

- accepting weak vendor staffing may reduce delivery capacity later;
- failing to secure clinical participation may produce workflow defects;
- ignoring integration uncertainty may create schedule and quality escalation;
- accepting an incomplete deliverable may increase testing defects;
- overly punitive accountability may reduce psychological safety and issue reporting;
- weak executive communication may reduce trust during the recovery chapter;
- disciplined remediation may protect quality but increase short-term schedule pressure.

No consequence should appear disconnected from prior evidence or decisions.

---

## 16. Stakeholder Behavior by Role

### Executive Sponsor

Seeks visible momentum and confidence. Trust increases when the learner communicates clearly and acts early. Trust declines when surprises emerge without warning.

### Clinical Lead

Protects patient safety, workflow practicality, and clinician capacity. Engagement improves when clinical constraints are respected and input is visibly incorporated.

### Technical Lead

Values architectural integrity, realistic dependencies, and timely decisions. Frustration increases when scope or executive messaging overrides technical evidence.

### Operations Lead

Focuses on operational disruption, support readiness, and ownership clarity. Confidence increases when transition implications are considered early.

### Product or Business Owner

Pushes for feature progress and stakeholder value. May create scope pressure when delivery becomes constrained.

### PMO Representative

Monitors adherence to governance, status quality, risk discipline, and decision traceability.

### Vendor Delivery Lead

Seeks flexibility and commercial progress. Behavior depends on the clarity and enforcement of commitments.

### Privacy, Security, or Compliance Lead

Focuses on evidence, controls, data handling, and regulatory obligations. Must not be treated as a late-stage reviewer.

Chapter Three should influence at least the following dimensions:

- schedule confidence;
- quality confidence;
- risk exposure;
- stakeholder trust;
- team health;
- vendor confidence;
- governance confidence;
- scope clarity;
- operational readiness.

## 18. Experience-Level Tailoring

### Explorer

- clearer evidence prompts;
- more explicit distinction between risk, issue, defect, and change;
- stronger mentor guidance before major decisions;
- visible acceptance criteria;
- reduced ambiguity in stakeholder requests.

### Practitioner

- moderate ambiguity;
- balanced coaching;
- incomplete but sufficient evidence;
- realistic stakeholder tension;
- delayed consequences with some warning signals.

### Leader

- reduced scaffolding;
- competing interpretations of evidence;
- stronger executive pressure;
- more subtle vendor and stakeholder incentives;
- higher consequence intensity;
- limited advance warning of delayed effects.

The core narrative must remain common across levels.

---

## 19. Chapter Exit States

### 19.1 Strong Execution Exit

- teams mobilized effectively;
- vendor expectations controlled;
- dependencies surfaced early;
- quality evidence used appropriately;
- first deliverable accepted or conditionally accepted with manageable remediation;
- stakeholder trust stable or improving.

### 19.2 Fragile Execution Exit

- delivery continues;
- some dependencies remain unresolved;
- remediation workload is significant;
- trust is mixed;
- schedule and quality confidence are weakened;
- Chapter Four begins with elevated recovery pressure.

### 19.3 Escalated Execution Exit

- first deliverable is rejected or materially incomplete;
- executive concern increases;
- vendor confidence declines;
- stakeholder conflict intensifies;
- formal recovery action is required at the start of Chapter Four.

All exit states must allow the simulation to continue while preserving consequences.

---

## 20. Transition to Chapter Four

Chapter Four begins when:

- required Chapter Three activities are complete;
- required decisions are resolved;
- the first major deliverable has an acceptance status;
- quality findings are documented;
- unresolved issues and risks have explicit owners;
- stakeholder reactions are recorded;
- corrective actions are carried forward;
- the chapter milestone is reached.

The transition must carry forward:

- accepted defects and remediation commitments;
- vendor performance concerns;
- unresolved integration risk;
- clinical engagement quality;
- executive trust;
- schedule and cost pressure;
- team health;
- governance credibility;
- all open decisions and commitments.

Chapter Four must feel like the consequence of Chapter Three, not a new scenario.
