## 1. Purpose

This document defines the canonical stakeholder model for the Northstar Connected Care flagship simulation. It establishes each stakeholder's role, objectives, motivations, concerns, communication style, influence, relationship trajectory, chapter-specific behavior, escalation triggers, trust progression, and response patterns.

All chapter specifications, meetings, inbox items, decision scenarios, documents, and delayed consequences must remain consistent with these arcs **and with the authoritative roster**.

---

## 2. Stakeholder Design Principles

### 2.1 Persistent actors

Stakeholders are not temporary content devices. They remember commitments, observe learner behavior, react to project conditions, and carry unresolved concerns across chapters.

### 2.2 Behavior changes through evidence

A stakeholder's behavior should change because of observable project events, learner decisions, communication quality, delivered outcomes, broken commitments, unresolved risks, or changes in organizational pressure.

### 2.3 No artificial hostility

Resistance must be grounded in role-based concerns, incentives, accountability, or prior experience. Stakeholders should not become antagonistic without a credible reason.

### 2.4 Multiple defensible perspectives

Clinical, operational, technical, financial, executive, regulatory, vendor, and customer stakeholders may evaluate the same decision differently. The simulation must present competing priorities without reducing every disagreement to a correct-versus-incorrect binary.

### 2.5 Trust is cumulative

Trust should reflect whether the learner:

- communicates early;
- follows through on commitments;
- uses evidence;
- includes affected stakeholders;
- escalates responsibly;
- avoids unsafe promises;
- owns mistakes;
- protects value and operational continuity.

### 2.6 Influence is contextual

Formal authority, subject-matter expertise, executive access, operational control, regulatory accountability, and coalition strength may all affect influence. Influence can rise or fall by chapter.

---

## 3. Canonical Relationship States

The narrative should support these relationship states:

- **Aligned** — stakeholder trusts the learner and actively supports the project.
- **Supportive** — stakeholder generally supports the learner but continues to test delivery credibility.
- **Neutral** — stakeholder remains professionally engaged but has not formed strong confidence.
- **Concerned** — stakeholder sees material risk and expects corrective action.
- **Resistant** — stakeholder withholds support, challenges decisions, or blocks progress within their authority.
- **Escalated** — stakeholder has formally raised concerns to executive, governance, regulatory, legal, or operational channels.

These states are narrative classifications. Later BC-006 phases may implement them as explicit or derived system state.

# 5. Elena Ramirez — Executive Sponsor

## 5.1 Role

Executive sponsor accountable for strategic authorization, executive alignment, funding protection, governance support, and realization of the initiative's intended organizational value.

## 5.2 Primary objectives

- deliver a credible connected-care capability;
- demonstrate strategic value to the executive committee;
- maintain organizational confidence;
- avoid uncontrolled cost or schedule deterioration;
- preserve patient, operational, and reputational safety;
- establish clear benefits ownership.

## 5.3 Motivations

Elena believes Northstar is strategically necessary and personally visible. She wants progress, but she also needs the project to withstand executive scrutiny.

## 5.4 Concerns

- unclear accountability;
- missed milestones;
- late executive surprises;
- weak governance;
- uncontrolled scope;
- stakeholder conflict reaching the executive committee;
- a go-live decision that creates avoidable harm.

## 5.5 Communication style

Direct, concise, outcome-focused, and politically aware. She expects recommendations, not raw problems without framing.

## 5.6 Influence

Very high throughout the simulation. Her willingness to sponsor, protect, challenge, or escalate materially changes project momentum.

## 5.7 Trust triggers

Trust increases when the learner:

- provides early warning;
- presents clear options and consequences;
- links decisions to value and risk;
- resolves cross-functional conflict;
- avoids overpromising;
- demonstrates control during recovery.

Trust declines when the learner:

- hides bad news;
- presents inconsistent status;
- bypasses governance;
- makes unsupported guarantees;
- fails to distinguish risk from certainty;
- allows preventable executive surprises.

## 5.8 Chapter arc

### Chapter 1 — Initiation

Elena is optimistic and grants the learner authority, but expects rapid establishment of governance and delivery discipline.

### Chapter 2 — Planning

She pushes for a credible integrated baseline and may pressure the learner to commit before all uncertainty is resolved. Strong learners frame assumptions and conditions rather than provide false certainty.

### Chapter 3 — Early Execution

She becomes more interested in evidence of progress and asks whether early delivery problems reflect normal mobilization or structural weakness.

### Chapter 4 — Mid-Project Recovery

Her confidence becomes conditional. She expects a concise recovery plan, ownership, and a defensible recommendation. Poor transparency can move her to formal escalation.

### Chapter 5 — Delivery and Readiness

She seeks a clear go-live recommendation and may favor speed because of executive expectations. She responds positively when the learner protects the organization from unsafe compression.

### Chapter 6 — Closure and Benefits Realization

She evaluates the learner on value, leadership, stakeholder confidence, disciplined closure, and whether benefits ownership is real rather than ceremonial.

## 5.9 Possible final states

- active executive advocate;
- satisfied but cautious sponsor;
- disappointed sponsor requiring post-project oversight;
- sponsor who formally questions the learner's leadership credibility.

## 20. Escalation Model

Stakeholder escalation should be triggered by credible conditions, including:

- repeated missed commitments;
- hidden or minimized risk;
- patient or operational safety exposure;
- unsupported compliance claims;
- unapproved financial commitments;
- uncontrolled scope change;
- misleading status reporting;
- unresolved ownership disputes;
- pressure to accept incomplete readiness;
- repeated exclusion from decisions within a stakeholder's accountability.

Escalation may take the form of:

- direct challenge to the learner;
- formal issue entry;
- governance escalation;
- sponsor escalation;
- executive committee escalation;
- regulatory or legal escalation;
- refusal to approve or accept;
- documented dissent.

---

## 21. Reconciliation and Recovery

A damaged relationship must remain recoverable when the learner:

- acknowledges the concern;
- accepts appropriate accountability;
- provides evidence;
- clarifies ownership;
- follows through on a corrective action;
- communicates before the next escalation;
- avoids retaliatory or dismissive behavior.

Recovery should be gradual. One apology should not immediately erase a pattern of broken trust.
