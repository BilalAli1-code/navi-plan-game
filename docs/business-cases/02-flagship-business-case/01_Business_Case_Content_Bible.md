## 3. Organization Background

Northstar Community Health Network is a mid-sized nonprofit healthcare organization serving a mixed urban and suburban population through 12 outpatient clinics.

Northstar employs approximately 1,800 people, including 250 clinical providers. It manages approximately 400,000 patient visits annually and operates centralized scheduling, finance, technology, compliance, procurement, patient-experience, and transformation functions.

Northstar’s mission is to improve equitable access to reliable, coordinated, community-based care. Its reputation depends on patient trust, clinical quality, affordability, workforce stability, and responsible stewardship of limited resources.

### 3.1 Organizational strengths

- strong community reputation;
- committed clinical and operational staff;
- executive willingness to invest in access improvement;
- established privacy, compliance, and clinical-governance functions;
- experienced clinic leaders;
- stable core clinical platform.

### 3.2 Organizational weaknesses

- inconsistent clinic workflows;
- fragmented scheduling tools and practices;
- limited enterprise reporting;
- uneven change-management capability;
- strained call-center operations;
- competing executive priorities;
- dependency on a small number of technology vendors;
- limited capacity for simultaneous transformation initiatives.

---

## 4. Market and Operating Conditions

Northstar faces:

- growing demand for outpatient services;
- increased patient expectations for digital access;
- workforce shortages;
- pressure to reduce avoidable appointment gaps;
- rising vendor and labor costs;
- stronger privacy and cybersecurity expectations;
- competition from retail, virtual-care, and larger health systems;
- payer pressure for improved access and measurable outcomes;
- public scrutiny regarding equitable access.

The organization cannot treat the program as a simple software installation. The initiative changes workflows, roles, reporting, patient communication, vendor responsibilities, and operating behavior across the network.

---

## 5. Central Business Problem

Northstar is experiencing:

- long appointment wait times;
- inconsistent scheduling rules;
- high call abandonment;
- avoidable appointment gaps;
- fragmented patient communication;
- staff frustration;
- limited visibility into access performance;
- declining patient satisfaction;
- uneven access across clinics and patient groups.

The executive team has approved the Connected Care Access Program, but major disagreements remain regarding scope, pace, vendor dependence, governance, rollout strategy, success measures, data readiness, staff adoption, and acceptable risk.

## 6. Financial Context

### 6.1 Approved funding

- platform and integration: $1.75 million;
- implementation services: $850,000;
- internal staffing and backfill: $650,000;
- workflow redesign and training: $500,000;
- data migration, testing, and quality: $350,000;
- change communication and patient outreach: $220,000;
- security, privacy, and compliance: $180,000;
- measurement and stabilization: $300,000;
- total approved budget: $4.8 million.

### 6.2 Contingency

A $480,000 executive contingency reserve is controlled by the steering committee. The learner may recommend its use but cannot authorize it independently.

### 6.3 Expected annual benefits

The approved business case estimates $3.2 million in annual value after stabilization through:

- reduced appointment leakage;
- improved schedule utilization;
- reduced avoidable call volume;
- lower manual rework;
- improved patient retention;
- better staff productivity;
- improved access reporting;
- reduced missed-opportunity costs.

The benefit estimate is not guaranteed. BC-002 must define the assumptions and evidence that can increase or reduce confidence in the estimate.

## 7. Governance Structure

### 7.1 Executive sponsor

The executive sponsor owns the business outcome, protects strategic alignment, and chairs major authorization decisions.

### 7.2 Steering committee

The steering committee includes executive, clinical, operational, financial, technology, privacy, and patient-experience representation.

It approves:

- major scope changes;
- contingency use;
- contract changes;
- rollout authorization;
- major risk acceptance;
- pause, redirection, or termination.

### 7.3 Program Director for Strategic Transformation

The learner’s manager provides program-level coordination, coaching, escalation support, and accountability.

### 7.4 Project manager

The learner leads day-to-day integration, evidence gathering, stakeholder engagement, planning, decision preparation, risk management, communication, and delivery coordination.

## 10. Six-Chapter Narrative Framework

### Chapter 1 — Orientation and Initial Problem

The learner inherits an approved but incompletely defined transformation initiative. Executive pressure favors rapid action, while initial evidence reveals inconsistent workflows and unclear success measures.

Required decisions:

1. establish the immediate project-management response;
2. choose the initial stakeholder and evidence-engagement plan;
3. recommend an initial delivery and discovery posture.

### Chapter 2 — Investigation and Stakeholder Tension

The learner discovers disagreement about root causes, scope, workflow variation, patient needs, technical readiness, and benefit assumptions.

Required decisions:

4. prioritize investigation and evidence collection;
5. respond to stakeholder conflict;
6. define the initial problem and value hypothesis.

### Chapter 3 — Strategic Direction and Commitment

Vendor, delivery, scope, governance, and rollout choices become urgent. Leadership expects a commitment.

Required decisions:

7. select the tailored delivery approach;
8. recommend scope and release priorities;
9. recommend vendor and procurement controls;
10. present the strategic direction to governance.

### Chapter 4 — Crisis and Unexpected Consequence

A major integration, privacy, data-quality, adoption, vendor, or readiness problem emerges. Earlier decisions affect severity and recovery options.

Required decisions:

11. contain and communicate the crisis;
12. choose the risk and technical response;
13. address ethics, privacy, or quality pressure;
14. reset stakeholder and team commitments.

### Chapter 5 — Recovery and Final Recommendation

The learner must stabilize delivery, reassess value, negotiate commitments, and prepare a recommendation.

Required decisions:

15. allocate recovery effort and contingency;
16. choose the revised rollout path;
17. recommend proceed, redirect, pause, or terminate.

### Chapter 6 — Outcome and Reflection

The simulation presents outcomes and requires accountability and learning transfer.

Required decisions:

18. deliver the final executive recommendation;
19. complete the final reflection and lessons response.

## 12. Content Fairness Rules

- Every required decision must have enough accessible evidence for a defensible choice.
- Hidden information must be discoverable through reasonable project-management action.
- No consequence may depend on an uncommunicated rule.
- Stakeholders may be biased, incomplete, political, or mistaken, but the simulation itself must remain fair.
- Documents, messages, meetings, and conversations must not duplicate the same information without narrative purpose.
- Later facts must be consistent with earlier evidence or clearly explained as new developments.
- Decision outcomes must preserve meaningful trade-offs rather than use one obviously correct answer.

## 13. Consequence Model

Every required decision will define:

- evidence available before the decision;
- options or response structure;
- intended trade-offs;
- immediate project-health changes;
- delayed project-health changes;
- stakeholder trust changes;
- team effects;
- competency effects;
- event and content unlocks;
- narrative effects;
- recovery opportunities;
- Decision Log language;
- learner-facing explanation timing.

## 14. Crisis Design Direction

The flagship crisis will combine multiple dimensions rather than functioning as a single technical failure.

The crisis design will include:

- a data-quality or integration defect;
- privacy or ethical implications;
- vendor accountability tension;
- operational and patient impact;
- executive pressure regarding disclosure and schedule;
- team workload and trust consequences;
- different severity based on earlier decisions.

BC-002 will define the canonical crisis trigger, variants, evidence, stakeholder reactions, and recovery branches.
